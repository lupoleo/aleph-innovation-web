# Gozo Risk Overview

This page summarises the environmental and community risks that matter most
for Gozo, and ranks them by how much an evidence-based monitoring system could
help. Sewage and marine discharges rank highest: they combine a documented
pattern of incidents, a direct effect on health and tourism, and almost no
public real-time data.

Explore every risk, its locations and the planned sensors on the
[interactive Gozo Risk Map](/gozo-risk-map/index.html).

## How the risks were ranked

Each risk is scored from 1 to 5 on three axes:

- **L — Likelihood:** how often the risk materialises today.
- **I — Impact:** the effect on the environment and on the community.
- **M — Monitorability:** how well low-cost sensors and public data can observe it.

`Priority = L × I × M`

The top of the list is therefore not simply "the worst risks", but the risks
where monitoring creates the most value. Scores are a first estimate from
public sources and are meant to be re-scored with the relevant authorities and
local councils.

## Water cycle

Gozo's water cycle is the island's most fragile system. Wastewater treatment
runs close to or above design capacity at summer peaks, the only aquifer is
losing water every year, and illegal slurry disposal can disrupt the treatment
plant.

### R1. Sewage overflows and marine outfalls

The Gozo Wastewater Treatment Plant at Ras il-Ħobż has operated since 2007
with a design capacity of 6,000 m³ per day, and has been reported to exceed it
at tourist peaks. Treated effluent leaves through a submarine outfall. Divers
and local media have repeatedly reported plumes after heavy rain and overflow
episodes along the coast, and the Water Services Corporation has announced a
major upgrade to double the plant's capacity.

**What monitoring adds:** continuous turbidity, conductivity and temperature
readings near the outfall and nearby bays, rainfall-triggered alerts, and an
agent that correlates rain, sewer load and beach readings. The aim is an
independent, timestamped answer to a simple question: was there a discharge,
when, and how large?

### R3. Illegal farm slurry and manure disposal

Liquid farm waste is sometimes dumped into the public sewer, especially during
heavy rain. Such incidents have put treatment works at risk and have
interrupted the supply of reclaimed "new water" to farmers. Gozo has no
dedicated manure treatment plant, and spreading untreated manure on fields is
still common.

**What monitoring adds:** sewer-side sensors for ammonium, conductivity and
flow at a few manholes upstream of the plant can detect slurry signatures
within minutes, supporting the operator's own efforts.

### R8. Harbour, boat and fuel pollution

Mġarr Harbour hosts the ferry terminal, the fishing fleet and fuel handling.
Enclosed bays such as Mġarr ix-Xini, a Natura 2000 area, fill with boats in
summer, bringing fuel, grey water, litter and anchor damage.

**What monitoring adds:** hydrocarbon and turbidity sensors in the harbour,
Sentinel-1 radar for oil slicks, and vessel tracking (AIS) to give context.

### R9. Groundwater over-abstraction and salinisation

Gozo's mean sea level aquifer is assessed as being in poor quantitative status,
losing around 1.2 million m³ a year, with a growing risk of salinisation.
Reverse osmosis at Ħondoq ir-Rummien gives Gozo drinking-water autonomy, but
adds energy use and brine discharge.

**What monitoring adds:** borehole level and conductivity loggers, and a view
of the balance between groundwater, desalinated water and reclaimed water.
This is harder to measure cheaply, so it is planned for a later phase.

## Coast, land and climate

Gozo's coast is where its economy and its ecology overlap. Bathing water,
seagrass, cliffs and valleys are what visitors come for, and they absorb the
pressure of building, storms and heat.

### R2. Bathing water quality

The number of Maltese bathing sites rated "excellent" has fallen over the past
decade, and Gozo has the lowest share of excellent sites in the country.
Official sampling is fortnightly during the season, with results published
days later, so a short rain-driven overflow can come and go between samples.

**What monitoring adds:** a daily swim-risk indicator for each beach, combining
rain, wind, currents, outfall signals and the latest laboratory result. This is
the most visible public product of the project.

### R4. Overdevelopment, construction dust and noise

Planning applications in Gozo rose sharply in the late 2010s, with many permits
replacing single houses with apartment blocks. The effects include changes to
village character, dust and noise, more sewage load (R1) and more runoff (R6).

**What monitoring adds:** PM10 and noise sensors near major sites, Sentinel-2
change detection of new built-up areas, and an agent that maps new planning
applications against sewer, road and water capacity.

### R5. Extreme heat

Malta set a new July temperature record of 43.3 °C in 2026. Older residents,
outdoor workers and farmers are most exposed, and heat also drives water and
electricity demand and marine heatwaves.

**What monitoring adds:** street-level temperature and humidity sensors in
Victoria and village cores, with heat-health alerts.

### R6. Flash flooding

Short, intense storms flood the valleys that drain into Marsalforn and Xlendi
and the low streets of Victoria. Runoff also carries soil, manure and sewage
into the bays, linking this risk to R1, R2 and R3.

**What monitoring adds:** rain gauges and water-level sensors in the main
valleys, a rain-to-runoff model and alerts for Civil Protection and local
councils.

### R12. Coastal erosion and seagrass loss

*Posidonia oceanica* meadows protect beaches and store carbon. They are damaged
by anchoring, turbidity, coastal works and warming.

**What monitoring adds:** yearly Sentinel-2 seagrass mapping, drone surveys of
key bays and anchoring pressure from vessel tracking.

### R13. Aquaculture effluent and sea slime

Fish farms off Malta's coast can produce surface slime that drifts into bays,
and the location of future aquaculture zones near Gozo has been debated.

**What monitoring adds:** Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-3 slick and chlorophyll
monitoring, plus citizen reports.

## Community and economy

Gozo's growth is real and fast, and it lands on a small, fixed territory. Most
community risks are about capacity: how many people, cars, bins and boats the
island can carry at peak.

### R7. Tourism carrying capacity

Gozo and Comino received nearly 590,000 tourists in 2025, and same-day visits
have grown strongly over the past decade. Hotspots include the Blue Lagoon,
Ramla, Dwejra, Xlendi, Marsalforn and Mġarr ix-Xini. Privacy-compliant crowd
counting has already been used successfully at Golden Bay in Malta.

**What monitoring adds:** real-time beach occupancy and "best time to visit"
guidance, and evidence for carrying-capacity planning.

### R10. Ferry and traffic emissions

Vehicle traffic across the Gozo Channel is high and ferry capacity is growing.
The only official air-quality station in Gozo is at Għarb, a rural background
site, so there is no measurement at Mġarr or in Victoria, where people and
traffic concentrate.

**What monitoring adds:** low-cost NO₂, PM2.5 and PM10 sensors at Mġarr, along
the Mġarr–Victoria road and in Victoria, calibrated against the reference
station. A baseline measured now is valuable whatever transport decisions are
taken later.

### R11. Seasonal waste

Short-let zones and bays generate large waste peaks in summer. Existing smart
bin pilots in Gozo can be integrated as a data feed rather than duplicated.

### R14. Demographic and social pressure

Gozo's population is projected to grow from about 40,000 in 2022 to over
50,000 by 2042, with a fast-growing share of foreign residents. Population
growth drives every other risk on this page. It is not measured by sensors: it
is used as a context layer to express indicators per resident and per visitor,
and it means public information must be multilingual.

## Balancing growth and protection

The Gozo Regional Development Strategy already aims to integrate economic
growth, environmental protection and social wellbeing. What is missing is
shared, trusted, near-real-time evidence. Each of the conflicts below is
argued today largely with anecdotes, and each can be informed by measurement.

| Conflict | Question monitoring can answer | Indicator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More tourism and short lets | At what visitor load do overflows and bathing-water failures start? | Overflow events vs visitors per day |
| More housing | Which localities still have sewer and road headroom? | Planned units vs network capacity |
| Faster links to Malta | What is the baseline before the change, and what changes after? | NO₂, PM and vehicle counts at Mġarr and Victoria |
| Intensive livestock | Where and when is illegal disposal happening? | Ammonium and conductivity spikes in sewers |
| Water supply growth | Is the aquifer recovering as desalination grows? | Borehole conductivity trend |
| Aquaculture | Does effluent reach Gozo's bays? | Satellite slick and chlorophyll |

The system does not decide what Gozo should do. It produces verified evidence,
states its uncertainty, and leaves decisions to people and institutions.

## Data gaps

Much of the data already exists, but it is scattered, delayed and mostly not
machine-readable. Three gaps matter most:

1. **No independent sea-side evidence of discharges** (R1, R3).
2. **No continuous bathing-water signal** between official samples (R2).
3. **No air-quality data where people and traffic concentrate** (R10).
